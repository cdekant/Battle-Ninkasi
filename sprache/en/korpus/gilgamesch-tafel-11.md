Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti, the Distant One:
(Gilgamesh:) "I look upon thee, Ūta-napišti,
thy form is not different, thou art as I,
thou art not different at all, thou art as I.
I was wholly bent on fighting thee,
but before thee my hand is stayed.
How camest thou into the assembly of the gods and foundest life?"
Ūta-napišti spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Ūta-napišti:) "I will reveal unto thee, Gilgamesh, a hidden thing,
a secret of the gods will I tell thee.
Šuruppak, a city that thou thyself knowest,
that lieth upon the bank of the Euphrates,
that city was old, and the gods were within it,
when the great gods resolved to send the Flood.
Their father Anu swore the oath,
their counsellor, the hero Enlil,
their chamberlain Ninurta,
their overseer of the canals Ennugi.
With them sat the prince Ea under the oath,
yet he told their words over to a fence of reeds:
'Reed fence, reed fence! Wall, wall!
Hear, reed fence! Mark, wall!
Man of Šuruppak, son of Ubār-Tutu,
tear down the house, build a boat!
Leave riches and seek life!
Spurn possessions and save life!
Bring the seed of all living things into the boat!
The boat that thou shalt build,
let her measures answer one another,
let breadth and length be equal,
roof her over like the Deep.'
I understood and spake unto Ea, my lord:
'I will do, my lord, what thou hast told me thus.
But what shall I answer the city, the folk and the elders?'
Ea opened his mouth and spake unto me, his servant:
'Thus shalt thou speak unto them:
surely Enlil hath cast hatred upon me,
I cannot dwell in your city,
I cannot tread upon the ground of Enlil.
I go down into the Deep, to dwell with Ea, my lord,
but he will rain plenty upon you,
a multitude of birds, a shoal of fishes,
riches at the time of harvest,
in the morning he will rain cakes upon you,
in the evening a rain of wheat.'"

(Ūta-napišti:) "At the first glimmer of dawn
the land gathered at the gate of Atra-ḫasīs.
The carpenter bore his axe,
the reed worker bore his stone,
the shipwright his heavy axe,
the old men bore ropes of palm fibre,
the rich man bore bitumen,
the poor man brought the tackle.
On the fifth day I set her hull:
an acre was her floor, ten rods high stood her walls,
ten rods round was the rim of her roof.
I set her body and drew her plan:
six decks I gave her,
into seven parts I divided her,
her inside I divided into nine.
I drove the water pegs into her belly,
I sought out a pole and set the tackle in place.
Three myriad measures of bitumen I poured into the kiln,
three myriad of asphalt I tipped therein,
three myriad of oil the porters hauled.
Oxen I slaughtered without number,
sheep I slaughtered day by day.
Beer, fine beer, oil and wine
I dealt out to the workmen like the water of the river,
they held a feast as on the days of the New Year!
At sunrise I set my hand to the anointing,
before sunset the boat was finished.
The slipway we shifted to and fro,
until two thirds of the boat were in the water.

All that I had I loaded aboard,
all the silver that I had I loaded aboard,
all the gold that I had I loaded aboard,
all the seed of living things that I had I loaded aboard.
All my kin I made go up into the boat,
the herds of the wild, the beasts of the wild, folk of every art and every craft.
Šamaš had set me a time:
'In the morning he raineth cakes, in the evening a rain of wheat,
then go into the boat and seal thy hatch!'
That time was come.
I looked upon the face of the weather,
the weather was fearful to behold,
I went into the boat and sealed my hatch.
To him that caulked the boat, to the shipwright Puzur-Enlil,
I gave the palace with all its goods.

At the first glimmer of dawn
there rose from the rim of heaven a black cloud,
within it Adad roared without ceasing.
Šullat and Ḫaniš went before,
throne bearers over mountain and land.
Errakal tore out the posts,
Ninurta went and made the weirs overflow.
The Anunnaki bore torches aloft
and set the land aglow with their radiance.
The stillness of the storm god passed over the sky,
all that was bright was turned to darkness.
He charged the land like a bull and broke it like a pot,
for one day the gale, the Flood rolled the land flat.
Swiftly it blew, then came the Flood,
like a battle destruction passed over the people.
None could see another,
the people knew not one another in the ruin.
Even the gods were afraid of the Flood,
they drew back and went up to the heaven of Anu.
The gods crouched like dogs, outside they lay stretched out.
The goddess cried out like a woman in travail,
Bēlet-ilī of the sweet voice lamented aloud:
'Truly, the days of old are turned to clay,
because I spake evil in the assembly of the gods.
How could I speak evil in the assembly of the gods
and declare war to destroy my people?
I myself gave birth to them, they are my people!
Now like the spawn of fish they fill the sea.'
The gods, the Anunnaki, wept with her,
wet with grief was their face, they wept with her,
their lips were parched, seized with fever.
Six days and seven nights
blew the wind, the Flood, the gale that cast down the land.
When the seventh day came,
the gale slackened, the Flood was ended.
The sea grew still that had fought like a woman in travail,
the tempest rested, the Flood was ended.

I looked upon the weather, and there was silence,
but all mankind was turned to clay.
The plain was level as a roof.
I opened a vent, and the light fell upon my cheek.
I sank upon my knees, sat there and wept,
the tears ran down my cheek.
I peered toward the shores, toward the rim of the sea,
in fourteen places land came forth.
On Mount Nimuš the boat ran aground,
Mount Nimuš held the boat fast and let it not sway,
one day, a second day, unto the sixth day Mount Nimuš held it fast.
When the seventh day came,
I brought out a dove and let it fly,
the dove flew away,
it found no place to rest and came back unto me.
I brought out a swallow and let it fly,
the swallow flew away,
it found no place to rest and came back unto me.
I brought out a raven and let it fly,
the raven flew away and saw the waters sinking,
it ate, it scratched, it pecked and came not back unto me.

I brought out an offering and sacrificed to the four winds,
I strewed incense upon the peak of the mountain.
Seven and seven libation vessels I set up,
beneath them I heaped reed, cedar and myrtle.
The gods smelled the savour,
the gods smelled the sweet savour,
the gods gathered like flies about him that sacrificed.
As soon as Bēlet-ilī came,
she lifted up the great flies that Anu had made her when he wooed her:
'Ye gods, let these be the lapis lazuli about my neck,
that I may remember these days and never forget them!
Let the gods come to the incense,
but let Enlil not come to the incense,
for he was without counsel and brought the Flood
and gave my people over to ruin.'
As soon as Enlil came,
Enlil saw the boat and was wroth,
he was full of anger at the Igigi gods:
'Whence hath this living thing escaped?
No man should have outlived the ruin!'
Ninurta opened his mouth and spake unto the hero Enlil:
'Who but Ea accomplisheth such things?
Ea alone knoweth every work.'
Ea opened his mouth and spake unto the hero Enlil:
'Thou, the sage of the gods, thou hero,
how couldst thou without counsel bring the Flood?
On him that sinneth lay his sin,
on him that transgresseth lay his transgression!
Slacken, lest it snap, pull, lest it go slack!
Instead of the Flood that thou broughtest
a lion might have risen and lessened mankind,
instead of the Flood a wolf might have risen and lessened mankind,
instead of the Flood a famine might have smitten the land,
instead of the Flood Erra might have risen and smitten the land!
I did not betray the secret of the great gods,
I let Atra-ḫasīs see a dream, so he heard the secret of the gods.
Now take counsel what shall be done with him.'
Enlil came up into the boat,
he took my hands and led me out,
he led out my wife and made her kneel at my side,
he touched our brows, stood between us and blessed us:
'Aforetime Ūta-napišti was a man,
but now shall Ūta-napišti and his wife be as we gods!
Ūta-napišti shall dwell afar, at the mouth of the rivers!'
They took me and made me dwell afar, at the mouth of the rivers.
But now, who will gather the gods for thee,
that thou mayest find the life thou seekest?
Up, for six days and seven nights sleep not!"

No sooner had he sat down upon his haunches
than sleep breathed over him like a mist.
Ūta-napišti spake unto her, unto his wife:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Behold the lad that asked for life,
sleep breatheth over him like a mist."
His wife spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti, the Distant One:
(Wife:) "Touch him, that the man may wake,
by the road he came let him return in safety,
through the gate he went out let him go home to his land!"
Ūta-napišti spake unto her, unto his wife:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Deceitful is mankind, he will deceive thee.
Go, bake him his bread day by day, set it in a row by his head
and mark upon the wall the days that he sleepeth."
She baked him his bread day by day, set it in a row by his head
and marked upon the wall the days that he slept.
His first loaf was dried up,
the second was leathery, the third was damp,
the fourth was turned white, the fifth bore a stain of mould,
the sixth was fresh baked,
the seventh still lay upon the embers, then he touched him and the man awoke.
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti, the Distant One:
(Gilgamesh:) "Scarce had sleep poured over me
when thou didst touch me and wake me!"
Ūta-napišti spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Come, Gilgamesh, count me thy loaves,
then shall it be known to thee how long thou sleptest."
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti, the Distant One:
(Gilgamesh:) "What shall I do, Ūta-napišti, whither shall I go?
The Robber hath seized my flesh,
in my bedchamber dwelleth Death,
and whithersoever I turn my face, there is Death."

Ūta-napišti spake unto him, unto Uršanabi the boatman:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Uršanabi, let the quay spurn thee, let the ferry scorn thee,
thou that walkedst upon its shore, go now without it!
The man whom thou hast led hither,
whose body is tousled with matted hair,
whose fair flesh the hides have spoiled,
take him, Uršanabi, and bring him to the washing place,
let him wash his matted hair clean,
let him cast off his hides, and let the sea bear them away,
let his fair body be bathed,
let the cloth of his head be renewed,
let him be clad in a royal robe, as befitteth his dignity!
Until he cometh unto his city,
until he reacheth the end of his road,
let the robe show no stain, but stay all new!"
Uršanabi took him and brought him to the washing place,
and all was done as Ūta-napišti had spoken.
Gilgamesh and Uršanabi went aboard the boat,
they pushed off the ship and sailed it themselves.
His wife spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti, the Distant One:
(Wife:) "Gilgamesh came hither, he toiled, he laboured,
what hast thou given him as he returneth to his land?"
But Gilgamesh lifted the pole
and brought the boat near unto the shore.
Ūta-napišti spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Thou camest hither, Gilgamesh, thou toiledst, thou labouredst,
what have I given thee as thou returnest to thy land?
I will reveal unto thee, Gilgamesh, a hidden thing,
a secret of the gods will I tell thee:
there is a plant, its look is like the boxthorn,
its thorn is like the wild rose's, it will prick thy hands.
If thou gettest this plant into thy hand, thou shalt find life."

When Gilgamesh heard this,
he opened a shaft,
heavy stones he bound upon his feet,
they drew him down into the Deep.
He plucked the plant, he plucked it and lifted it up,
he cut the heavy stones from his feet,
the sea cast him up upon its shore.
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Uršanabi the boatman:
(Gilgamesh:) "Uršanabi, this plant is the plant of the heartbeat,
whereby a man winneth back his strength of life.
I will bring it to Uruk the Fold,
an old man will I let eat of it and put the plant to the test.
If the old man groweth young again,
then I myself will eat of it and be as I was in my youth!"
At twenty leagues they broke bread,
at thirty leagues they pitched the night camp.
Gilgamesh found a pool whose water was cool,
he went down to bathe in the water.
A serpent smelled the fragrance of the plant,
silently it came up and bore the plant away,
and as it turned away, it cast its skin.
Then Gilgamesh sat down and wept,
the tears ran down his cheek.
He spake unto Uršanabi the boatman:
(Gilgamesh:) "For whom, Uršanabi, have my arms grown weary,
for whom hath the blood of my heart run dry?
Not for myself have I done good,
for the Lion of the Earth have I done good.
Now the tide riseth already twenty leagues,
when I opened the shaft I left the tools behind.
What shall I find to serve me for a token?
Had I but turned away and left the boat upon the shore!"

At twenty leagues they broke bread,
at thirty leagues they pitched the night camp,
then they came unto Uruk the Fold.
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Uršanabi the boatman:
(Gilgamesh:) "Go up upon the wall of Uruk, Uršanabi, and walk about,
search out the footing and look upon the brickwork:
is not its brickwork of burnt brick,
and did not the Seven Sages lay its ground?
One šar is city, one šar palm grove, one šar clay pit, half a šar the house of Ištar:
three šar and a half is Uruk in its compass."
