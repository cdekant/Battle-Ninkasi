Šiduri was an alewife that dwelt upon the shore of the sea,
there she dwelt in a tavern by the sea.
Jar stands she had and vats of gold,
in cloths was she wrapped and veiled with veils.
Gilgamesh came wandering,
clad in a pelt and fearful to behold.
He bore the flesh of the gods in his body,
but grief was in his heart,
his face was like the face of one who cometh from far roads.
The alewife saw him from afar,
she spake a word within herself
and took counsel with her own heart:
(Šiduri:) "Surely this one is a slayer of wild bulls,
whence cometh he straight toward my gate?"
The alewife saw him and barred her gate,
she barred her gate and went up upon the roof.
But he, Gilgamesh, had keen hearing,
he lifted his chin and turned toward her.
Gilgamesh spake unto her, unto the alewife:
(Gilgamesh:) "Alewife, why didst thou bar thy gate when thou sawest me?
Thou didst bar thy gate and go up upon the roof.
I will smite the door, I will shatter the bolt!"
The alewife spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Šiduri:) "Therefore I barred my gate, therefore I went up upon the roof.
Let me know whence thy road cometh."
Gilgamesh spake unto her, unto the alewife:
(Gilgamesh:) "My friend Enkidu and I,
it was we that joined together and climbed the mountain land,
seized the Bull of Heaven and slew the Bull of Heaven,
destroyed Ḫumbaba, who dwelt in the Cedar Forest,
and killed the lions in the passes of the mountains."

The alewife spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Šiduri:) "If thou and Enkidu were they that slew the warden,
that destroyed Ḫumbaba, who dwelt in the Cedar Forest,
that killed the lions in the passes of the mountains,
that seized and slew the Bull of Heaven that came down from heaven,
why are thy cheeks hollow, thy face sunken,
thy spirit wretched, thy features wasted?
Why is there grief in thy heart,
is thy face like the face of one who cometh from far roads?
Why is thy face burnt by frost and the glare of the sun,
and why roamest thou the wild, decked out like a lion?"
Gilgamesh spake unto her, unto the alewife:
(Gilgamesh:) "How should my cheeks not be hollow, my face not sunken,
my spirit not wretched, my features not wasted?
How should there not be grief in my heart,
my face not be like the face of one who cometh from far roads?
How should my face not be burnt by frost and the glare of the sun,
and how should I not roam the wild, decked out like a lion?
My friend, fleeing mule, wild ass of the mountains, panther of the wild,
my friend whom I loved so dearly, who went with me through every peril,
my friend Enkidu whom I loved so dearly, who went with me through every peril,
the fate of mankind hath overtaken him.
Six days and seven nights I wept over him,
I gave him not up for burial:
'Perhaps my friend will rise at my cry!'
until a worm fell from his nose.
Then I feared that I too should die,
death I feared, therefore I roam the wild.
The lot of my friend lieth too heavy upon me,
therefore I roam the wild upon a far road.
How could I be silent, how could I be still?
My friend whom I loved is turned to clay,
my friend Enkidu whom I loved is turned to clay.
Shall not I also lie down like him
and not rise again for all eternity?
Now, alewife, I have seen thy face,
but death, that I ever fear, I would not see."

The alewife spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Šiduri:) "Gilgamesh, whither runnest thou?
The life that thou seekest thou shalt not find.
When the gods made mankind,
they allotted death unto mankind,
life they kept in their own hands.
Thou, Gilgamesh, fill thy belly,
make merry by day and by night!
Make thee a feast of joy every day,
dance and play by day and by night!
Let thy garments be clean,
let thy head be washed, in water shalt thou bathe!
Look upon the little one that holdeth thy hand,
let thy wife delight in thy lap again and again!
Such is the lot of mankind."
Gilgamesh spake unto her, unto the alewife:
(Gilgamesh:) "What sayest thou, my alewife?
My heart is sick for my friend,
my heart is sick for Enkidu.
Thou dwellest, alewife, upon the shore of the sea,
thou knowest all the ways.
Now, alewife, which is the road to Ūta-napišti?
What is its token? Give it me!
Give me, I pray, its token!
If it may be, I will cross the sea,
if it may not be, I will roam the wild!"

The alewife spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Šiduri:) "Never was there, Gilgamesh, a crossing,
and from days of old none that crossed the sea.
He that crosseth the sea is Šamaš the hero,
save Šamaš, who can cross the sea?
The crossing is toilsome, its way full of peril,
and between lie the Waters of Death that bar the way before.
And when thou, Gilgamesh, hast crossed the sea,
what wilt thou do when thou comest to the Waters of Death?
Gilgamesh, there is Uršanabi, the boatman of Ūta-napišti,
and the Stone Ones are with him, he strippeth a pine in the midst of the forest.
Go and let him see thy face!
If it may be, cross over with him,
if it may not be, turn back!"

When Gilgamesh heard this,
he took the axe in his hand,
drew the dagger from his belt,
crept down and rushed upon them.
Like an arrow he fell among them,
his shout boomed through the midst of the forest.
Uršanabi saw him and wrapped himself in his radiance,
he took an axe and trembled before him.
But Gilgamesh smote him upon the head and seized him by the hair,
he pinned his arm against his breast.
The Stone Ones had made the boat tight,
they that feared not the Waters of Death.
Gilgamesh strode into the sea, the wide ocean,
in the water he stopped the boat and the Stone Ones,
he shattered the Stone Ones and tipped them into the stream,
he bound the boat fast with a rope
and sat down upon the bank.
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Uršanabi the boatman:
(Gilgamesh:) "Now, Uršanabi, thou tookest an axe and didst tremble before me!
The fight and the battle thou soughtest I will not wage against thee."
Uršanabi asked him as the alewife had asked him,
and Gilgamesh answered him as he had answered her:
(Gilgamesh:) "My friend whom I loved is turned to clay.
Now, Uršanabi, which is the road to Ūta-napišti?"
Uršanabi spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Uršanabi:) "Thine own hands, Gilgamesh, have hindered thy crossing.
Thou hast shattered the Stone Ones and tipped them into the stream,
the Stone Ones are shattered, and the cedar is not stripped.
Take, Gilgamesh, the axe in thy hand,
go down into the forest and cut me three hundred punting poles, each five rods long,
smooth them and furnish them with knobs."
When Gilgamesh heard this,
he went down into the forest and cut him three hundred punting poles, each five rods long,
he smoothed them and furnished them with knobs.

Gilgamesh and Uršanabi went aboard the boat,
they pushed off the ship and sailed it themselves.
By the third day they had gone a road of a month and a half,
then Uršanabi came unto the Waters of Death.
Uršanabi spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Uršanabi:) "Take, Gilgamesh, the first pole!
Let not thy hand be touched by the Waters of Death, else it will be lamed!
Take a second, a third and a fourth pole, Gilgamesh,
take the fifth unto the twelfth pole, Gilgamesh!"
After a hundred and twenty double leagues Gilgamesh had used up the poles.
Then Uršanabi loosed his clothes,
Gilgamesh stripped off his garment,
with his arms he made a high yard.

Ūta-napišti saw him from afar,
he spake a word within himself
and took counsel with his own heart:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Why are the Stone Ones of the boat shattered,
and aboard is one that is not its master?
He that cometh is none of my folk.
I look, he is none of my folk."
Gilgamesh drew nigh unto the quay
and spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti:
(Gilgamesh:) "Long live Ūta-napišti, the son of Ubār-Tutu!"
Ūta-napišti asked him as the alewife and the boatman had asked him,
and Gilgamesh answered him as he had answered them.
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto Ūta-napišti:
(Gilgamesh:) "I thought: I will go and find Ūta-napišti, the Distant One, of whom men speak.
So I went about through all the lands,
I went again and again over toilsome mountains,
I crossed again and again all the seas.
My face had not its fill of sweet sleep,
I tormented myself with waking,
I filled my sinews with pain.
What have I gained by my toil?
Ere I reached the alewife my garment was worn through.
I slew bear, hyena, lion, panther and cheetah,
stag, ibex, the herds and the game of the wild,
to eat their flesh and strip their hides.
Let them bar the gate of grief,
with pitch and bitumen let them seal its entrance!
For my sake let them break off the dance no more."

Ūta-napišti spake unto him, unto Gilgamesh:
(Ūta-napišti:) "Why, Gilgamesh, followest thou grief, the daughter of Anu?
Thou, mingled of the flesh of gods and men,
whom they made like thy father and thy mother!
Did ever, Gilgamesh, a palace get built for the fool,
a throne set for him in the council, and was he told: Sit thee down!?
To the fool they give the dregs of beer instead of ghee,
bran and grist instead of fine flour,
he is clad in a rag instead of fine garments,
instead of a belt he is girt with old rope,
for he hath no counsellors to guide him,
he hath no words of counsel.
But thou hast toiled, and what hast thou gained?
Thou wearest thyself out with restless toil,
thou fillest thy sinews with pain
and bringest nearer the end of thy life.
Man, whose name is snapped off like a reed in the canebrake,
the fair youth, the fair maiden,
death carrieth off all too soon.
None seeth death,
none seeth the face of death,
none heareth the voice of death,
and yet it is grim death that snappeth off mankind.
At some time we build a house,
at some time we found a nest,
at some time the brothers share the inheritance,
at some time strife ariseth in the land.
At some time the river rose and brought the flood,
the mayfly floated upon the river,
its face gazed into the face of the sun,
and all at once there was nothing!
The carried off and the dead, how like they are to each other!
Of death no image hath been drawn,
no dead man hath ever greeted a man in the land.
The Anunnaki, the great gods, were assembled,
Mammītum, who maketh destiny, decreed the lot with them:
death and life they set down,
but the day of death they made not known."
