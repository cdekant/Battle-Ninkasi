He who beheld the Deep, the ground of the land,
who knew the paths, was skilled in all things,
Gilgamesh, who beheld the Deep, the ground of the land,
who knew the paths, was skilled in all things:
he searched out in every place the thrones of the mighty,
the wisdom of all things he knew whole and entire.
He beheld the hidden and laid bare the veiled,
tidings he brought down from the days before the Flood.
A far road he went, grew weary and came to rest,
all his toil is written upon a stone.

He built the wall of Uruk the Fold,
of holy Eanna, the pure storehouse.
Behold its wall, that windeth like a thread of wool,
look upon its battlement, whose like none buildeth!
Take the stair that hath stood since days of old
and draw nigh unto Eanna, the seat of Ištar,
whose like no later king shall ever build.
Go up upon the wall of Uruk and walk about,
search out the footing and look upon the brickwork:
is not its brickwork of burnt brick,
and did not the Seven Sages lay its ground?
One šar is city, one šar palm grove, one šar clay pit, half a šar the house of Ištar:
three šar and a half is Uruk in its compass.

Open the chest of cedar wood,
loose its bolts of bronze,
lift the lid of its secret,
take the tablet of lapis lazuli and read aloud
of all the woe that Gilgamesh wandered through.

Exalted above kings, a lord of noble form,
the hero, Uruk's offspring, a goring wild bull!
He goeth before, the first of all,
he goeth behind, the stay of his brothers.
A mighty dyke, the shelter of his men,
a raging wave that shattereth walls of stone!
Wild bull of Lugalbanda, Gilgamesh, perfect in strength,
suckled by the lofty cow, the Wild Cow Ninsun!
Tall is Gilgamesh, perfect and fearsome,
who opened the passes in the mountains,
who dug wells upon the flanks of the hills,
who crossed the sea, the wide ocean, unto the rising of the sun,
who roamed the quarters of the world, ever seeking life,
who reached by his strength Ūta-napišti, the Distant One,
who brought back to their places the shrines the Flood had ruined
and set for mankind the rightful rites.
Who is his equal in kingship,
who can speak like Gilgamesh: "I am the king!"?

Gilgamesh was his name from the day of his birth,
two thirds of him god and one third man.
Bēlet-ilī drew the likeness of his body,
Nudimmud perfected his form.
Mighty was his stature, eleven cubits he measured,
four cubits broad was his breast,
three cubits long his foot, half a rod his leg,
six cubits his stride,
three cubits the locks upon his cheeks.
Bearded were his cheeks, dark as lapis lazuli,
the hair of his head grew thick as the grain of Nisaba.
When he grew up he was perfect in beauty,
by the measure of the earth he was glorious to behold.

In the Fold of Uruk he goeth about,
he lordeth it like a wild bull, his head held high.
None is his match, his weapons are ever raised,
at the game of pukku he keepeth his fellows on their feet.
The young men of Uruk are vexed without cause,
Gilgamesh letteth no son go to his father.
By day and by night he rageth in his pride,
Gilgamesh, the king of the wide multitude,
yet he is the shepherd of Uruk the Fold!
Gilgamesh letteth no daughter go to her mother,
he is their bull and they are the cows.
Strong he is, glorious and knowing,
yet Gilgamesh letteth no bride go to her bridegroom.
The warrior's daughter, the young man's bride,
their lament was heard by the goddess Ištar.

The gods of heaven, the lords of counsel, cried unto Anu:
"Hast thou begotten a savage bull in Uruk the Fold?
None is his match, his weapons are ever raised,
at the game of pukku he keepeth his fellows on their feet.
The young men of Uruk he vexeth without cause,
Gilgamesh letteth no son go to his father.
By day and by night he rageth in his pride,
yet he is the shepherd of Uruk the Fold!
Gilgamesh, the keeper of the wide multitude,
he is their shepherd and they are his flock.
Strong he is, glorious and knowing,
yet Gilgamesh letteth no bride go to her bridegroom."
The warrior's daughter, the young man's bride,
their lament was heard by Anu.

They called Aruru, the great one:
"Thou, Aruru, didst make mankind,
now make what he deviseth!
Let him be a match for the storm of his heart,
let them strive one with the other, and Uruk shall have rest."
When Aruru heard this,
she shaped in her heart what Anu devised.
Aruru washed her hands,
pinched off a piece of clay and cast it out into the wild.
In the wild she made Enkidu, the hero,
a child of silence, knit together by Ninurta.
Shaggy is his body with hair,
he weareth long locks like a woman,
the hair of his head groweth thick as the grain of Nisaba.
He knoweth neither folk nor land,
he is clad like Šakkan, the lord of beasts.
With the gazelles he grazeth on herbs,
with the herd he jostleth at the watering place,
with the beasts his heart delighteth in the water.

A hunter, a setter of snares,
met him face to face at the watering place.
One day, a second and a third he met him face to face at the watering place.
The hunter saw him, and his face went stiff,
but Enkidu went home with his herd unto his lair.
The hunter was afraid, grew still and fell silent,
his heart was troubled, his face darkened,
grief was in his inward parts,
his face was like the face of one who cometh from far roads.
The hunter opened his mouth and spake unto his father:
(Hunter:) "My father, a man is come unto us,
the strongest in the land, strength hath he,
his strength is mighty as a lump from the heaven of Anu.
Ever he roameth over the hills,
ever I see him with the herd,
ever his feet stand at the watering place.
I am afraid and dare not draw near him.
He hath filled the pits that I dug,
he hath torn up the nets that I laid,
he letteth the herd, the beasts of the wild, escape from my hands,
he letteth me not do the work of the wild."

His father opened his mouth and spake unto the hunter:
(Father:) "Go, my son, unto Gilgamesh, the offspring of Uruk's midst,
step before him,
his strength is mighty as a lump from the heaven of Anu.
Take the road, set thy face toward Uruk.
Go, my son, and lead with thee Šamḫat, the harlot,
her charm is a match even for the strong.
When the herd cometh down to the watering place,
let her strip off her garment and show her fullness.
He will see her and draw near unto her,
then his herd will grow strange to him, with whom he grew up in the wild."

Heeding the counsel of his father
the hunter went his way,
he took the road and set his face toward Uruk,
unto Gilgamesh the king he repeated the words:
(Hunter:) "A man is come unto us,
the strongest in the land, strength hath he,
his strength is mighty as a lump from the heaven of Anu.
Ever he roameth over the hills,
ever I see him with the herd,
ever his feet stand at the watering place.
I am afraid and dare not draw near him.
He hath filled the pits that I dug,
he hath torn up the nets that I laid,
he letteth the herd, the beasts of the wild, escape from my hands,
he letteth me not do the work of the wild."
Gilgamesh spake unto him, unto the hunter:
(Gilgamesh:) "Go, hunter, and lead with thee Šamḫat, the harlot.
When the herd cometh down to the watering place,
let her strip off her garment and show her fullness.
He will see her and draw near unto her,
then his herd will grow strange to him, with whom he grew up in the wild."

The hunter went and led with him Šamḫat, the harlot,
they took the road and set out upon the journey.
On the third day they came unto the place,
the hunter and the harlot sat down to lie in wait.
One day, a second day they sat at the watering place,
then came the herd to drink at the watering place,
then came the beasts, their hearts delighted in the water,
and he also, Enkidu, whose home is the hills.
With the gazelles he grazeth on herbs,
with the herd he jostleth at the watering place,
with the beasts his heart delighteth in the water.

Šamḫat saw him, the primal man,
the ravening man from the depths of the wild.
(Hunter:) "There he is, Šamḫat! Loose thy breasts,
open thy lap, that he may take thy fullness!
Be not shy, take in his breath!
He will see thee and draw near unto thee.
Spread thy garment, that he may lie upon thee,
do unto the primal man the work of woman!
Then his herd will grow strange to him, with whom he grew up in the wild,
and his desire will clasp thee and caress thee."
Šamḫat loosed her skirt,
opened her lap, and he took her fullness.
She was not shy, she took in his breath,
she spread her garment, and he lay upon her.
She did unto the primal man the work of woman,
his desire clasped and caressed her.
Six days and seven nights was Enkidu roused and lay with Šamḫat.

When he was sated with her delights,
he turned his face toward his herd.
The gazelles saw Enkidu and ran away,
the herd of the wild drew back from his body.
Enkidu had sullied his pure body,
his knees stood still as his herd went off.
Enkidu was weakened, his running was not as before,
but now he had reason and a wide mind.
He turned back and sat at the feet of the harlot,
he gazed into the harlot's face,
and his ears heard what the harlot spake.

The harlot spake unto him, unto Enkidu:
(Šamḫat:) "Fair art thou, Enkidu, like unto a god,
why dost thou roam the wild with the beasts?
Come, I will lead thee to Uruk the Fold,
to the pure house, the dwelling of Anu and Ištar,
to holy Eanna, the dwelling of Ištar,
where Gilgamesh is, perfect in strength,
and lordeth it over the men like a wild bull."
She spake unto him, and her word pleased him,
his heart, now knowing, sought a friend.
Enkidu spake unto her, unto the harlot:
(Enkidu:) "Come, Šamḫat, take me along
to the pure and holy house, the dwelling of Anu and Ištar,
where Gilgamesh is, perfect in strength,
and lordeth it over the men like a wild bull.
I will challenge him, for my strength is mighty,
I will boast in Uruk: I am the strongest!
There I will change what is decreed:
he that is born in the wild is mighty and hath strength."

(Šamḫat:) "Let the people see thy face.
Go, Enkidu, to Uruk the Fold,
where the young men are adorned with girdles.
Every day a feast is held there,
where the drums are beaten without end,
and the maidens are fair of form,
adorned with charm and full of joy,
even the old they fetch out of their beds!
Enkidu, thou that knowest not yet life,
I will show thee Gilgamesh, the merry man.
Look at him, behold his face:
fair is he in manhood, dignity hath he,
adorned with charm is all his body.
Mightier is his strength than thine,
he sleepeth not by day nor by night.
Enkidu, put away thine evil purpose,
for Gilgamesh is loved of Šamaš,
Anu, Enlil and Ea have widened his mind.
Ere ever thou camest from the hills,
Gilgamesh in Uruk saw thee in his dreams.

Gilgamesh arose to read his dream and spake unto his mother:
(Gilgamesh:) 'My mother, the dream that I saw this night:
the stars of heaven appeared unto me,
like a lump from the heaven of Anu one fell down upon me.
I lifted it, but it was too heavy for me,
I rolled it, but I could not move it.
The land of Uruk stood about it,
the land was gathered about it,
the throng pressed before it,
the men crowded about it,
as to a little child they kissed its feet.
I loved it as a wife and caressed it,
I lifted it and laid it at thy feet,
and thou, thou madest it my equal.'
The mother of Gilgamesh, clever and wise, who knoweth all things, spake unto her son,
the Wild Cow Ninsun, clever and wise, who knoweth all things, spake unto Gilgamesh:
(Ninsun:) 'The stars of heaven appeared unto thee,
like a lump from the heaven of Anu one fell down upon thee.
Thou didst lift it, but it was too heavy for thee,
thou didst roll it, but thou couldst not move it.
Thou didst lift it and lay it at my feet,
and I, I made it thine equal,
thou didst love it as a wife and caress it.
A strong companion shall come unto thee, who saveth his friend,
the strongest in the land, strength hath he,
his strength is mighty as a lump from the heaven of Anu.
Thou shalt love him as a wife and caress him,
and he, the strong one, shall save thee again and again.'

A second dream he saw,
he arose and went in before the goddess, his mother.
Gilgamesh spake unto her, unto his mother:
(Gilgamesh:) 'Again, my mother, I saw a second dream:
in a lane of Uruk, the broad market,
there lay an axe, and folk were gathered about it.
The land of Uruk stood about it,
the land was gathered about it,
the throng pressed before it,
the men crowded about it.
I lifted it and laid it at thy feet,
I loved it as a wife and caressed it,
and thou, thou madest it my equal.'
The mother of Gilgamesh, clever and wise, who knoweth all things, spake unto her son,
the Wild Cow Ninsun, clever and wise, who knoweth all things, spake unto Gilgamesh:
(Ninsun:) 'My son, the axe that thou sawest is a man.
Thou shalt love him as a wife and caress him,
and I, I make him thine equal.
A strong companion shall come unto thee, who saveth his friend,
the strongest in the land, strength hath he,
his strength is mighty as a lump from the heaven of Anu.'
Gilgamesh spake unto her, unto his mother:
(Gilgamesh:) 'My mother, by the word of Enlil, the great counsellor, may it befall me!
A friend, a counsellor will I gain,
gain will I a friend, a counsellor!'
Thus hath he seen his dreams."

After Šamḫat had told Enkidu the dreams of Gilgamesh,
the two of them made love together.
