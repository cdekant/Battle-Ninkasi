(Enkidu:) "My friend, wherefore did the great gods take counsel?"

Enkidu lifted up his eyes
and spake with the door as though it were a man:
(Enkidu:) "Door of the woodland, thou hast no understanding,
I have understanding that thou hast not.
Twenty leagues I sought for thee the finest timber,
until in the forest I found a lofty cedar.
Thy tree had not its equal in the Cedar Forest,
six rods art thou high, two rods broad, one cubit thick,
hinge, pivot and sill are all of one piece.
I made thee, I raised thee up, in Nippur I hung thee.
Had I known, door, that this would be thy thanks,
had I known, door, that this would be thy gift,
I would have taken the axe and hewn thee down,
upon a raft I would have brought thee to Ebabbar,
to Ebabbar, the house of Šamaš, would I have brought thee,
the cedar would I have set up in the gate of Ebabbar,
in its entrance would I have set the lion eagle and the bull colossus,
for Šamaš heard what I spake
and gave me a weapon in my need.
Now, door, it was I that made thee, that raised thee up!
I can curse thee too, I can tear thee down too!
Let a king that cometh after me abhor thee,
let him blot out my name and set his own upon thee!"
He tore off his garments and cast away his finery.

When Gilgamesh heard the words of Enkidu, his friend,
forthwith his tears did flow.
Gilgamesh opened his mouth and spake unto Enkidu:
(Gilgamesh:) "Thou that hadst understanding and reason, speakest thou now madness?
Why, my friend, did thy heart speak blasphemy?
The dream was precious and the dread was great,
thy fevered lips buzzed like flies,
great was the fear, rare the dream.
To him that liveth on is left the grief,
the dead leaveth sorrow to the living.
I will beseech the great gods and besiege them,
I will seek out Šamaš, I will pray to thy god.
Before thee will I pray to Anu, the father of the gods,
let Enlil, the great counsellor, hear my prayer before thee,
let my plea find grace before Ea!
An image of thee will I make of gold beyond measure."
(Enkidu:) "My friend, give no silver, give no gold,
the word that Enlil spake the gods cannot unsay.
What he commanded he blotteth not out again,
no wedge that he writeth doth he erase.
My friend, my lot is drawn,
men go before their time unto their fate."

At the first glimmer of dawn
Enkidu lifted his head and lamented before Šamaš,
in the glare of the sun his tears did flow:
(Enkidu:) "I call upon thee, Šamaš, for the sake of my precious life!
That hunter, the setter of snares,
who let me not be a match for my friend,
let the hunter not be a match for his friend!
Destroy his gain, lessen his wage,
let his share be cut before thee!
From the house he entereth let his god flee through the window!"
When he had cursed the hunter to his heart's content,
he resolved to curse the harlot Šamḫat also:
(Enkidu:) "Come, Šamḫat, I will decree thee a lot,
a lot that shall not end for all eternity!
I curse thee with a great curse,
and my curses shall strike thee, swiftly and soon!
No house shalt thou found wherein to rejoice,
never shalt thou dwell amid a family,
never shalt thou sit upon the chair of the young wives!
Let the ground befoul thy fair garment,
let the drunkard smear thy festive gown with dust!
Let the potter's clay pit serve thee for a home,
neither chamber nor house altar nor hearth shalt thou have!
No bed, no chair, no table that folk take pride in shall stand in thy chamber,
let the bench be the couch wherein thou rejoicest!
Let the crossing of the roads be thy seat,
the ruined houses thy sleeping place, the shadow of the city wall thy standing place!
Let thorn and thistle flay thy feet,
let the drunken and the sober smite thee upon the cheek!
Let the mason not plaster the roof of thy house,
in thy chamber let the owl dwell,
never in thy house shall a feast be held!
For thou hast made me weak, me that was pure,
me that was pure thou hast made weak in the wild."

Šamaš heard what he spake,
forthwith a voice cried unto him from heaven:
(Šamaš:) "Why, Enkidu, dost thou curse the harlot Šamḫat,
who gave thee bread to eat that befitteth a god,
who gave thee beer to drink that befitteth a king,
who clothed thee in a great garment
and gave thee the glorious Gilgamesh for a comrade?
Now Gilgamesh, thy friend and brother,
will lay thee upon a great bed,
upon a bed of honour will he lay thee,
he will set thee upon a quiet seat, the seat at his left,
the princes of the earth will kiss thy feet.
He will make the folk of Uruk weep and sob for thee,
the joyful folk will he fill with grief for thee.
And he, when thou art gone, will wear the matted hair of mourning,
he will wrap himself in a lion's skin and roam the wild."
Enkidu heard the words of Šamaš the hero,
his angry heart came to rest,
his raging heart came to rest.
(Enkidu:) "Come, Šamḫat, I will decree thee a lot,
my mouth that cursed thee shall turn and bless thee:
let governors and nobles love thee,
he that is a league away shall slap his thigh,
he that is two leagues away shall shake his locks,
let no soldier be slow to loose his belt for thee!
Obsidian, lapis lazuli and gold shall they give thee,
earrings in plenty be thy gift!
To a man whose house is well ordered, whose granaries are heaped high,
let Ištar, the ablest of the gods, lead thee in!
For thy sake let the first wife be forsaken, the mother of seven!"

But Enkidu was troubled in his mind,
he lay alone and pondered,
what lay upon his mind he told his friend:
(Enkidu:) "What a dream, my friend, that I saw this night!
The heavens thundered, the earth gave answer,
and I stood between them.
There was a man, grim was his look,
his face was like the face of the Anzû bird,
his hands were lion's paws, his nails eagle's talons,
he seized me by the locks, he was too strong for me.
I smote him, but he sprang back like a skipping rope,
he smote me and overturned me like a raft,
like a mighty wild bull he trampled over me
and drenched my body with poisonous slaver.
'Save me, my friend!'
But thou wast afraid of him.
He smote me and turned me into a dove,
he bound my arms like the wings of a bird,
to lead me captive to the darksome house, the seat of Irkalla,
unto the house whence none goeth forth that went therein,
unto the path whose going knoweth no turning,
unto the house whose dwellers are bereft of light,
where dust is their meal and clay their bread.
Feathered are they as birds, with wings for their raiment,
light they behold never, in darkness they crouch,
upon door and bolt the dust lieth heaped,
over the House of Dust the stillness of death is poured.
In the House of Dust that I entered,
I looked, and the crowns lay put away:
there sat the kings, the crowned ones, who ruled the land from days of old,
who set roast meat upon the tables of Anu and Enlil,
who set out baked bread and poured cool water from skins.
In the House of Dust that I entered,
there sat en priests and lagar priests,
there sat purification priests and lumaḫḫu priests,
there sat the gudapsû priests of the great gods,
there sat Etana, there sat Šakkan,
there sat the queen of the netherworld, Ereškigal.
Before her crouched Bēlet-ṣēri, the scribe of the netherworld,
she held a tablet and read aloud before her.
She lifted her head and saw me:
'Who hath fetched this man hither?
Who hath brought this fellow hither?'
I that bore all hardship with thee,
remember me, my friend, forget not all that I went through!"
(Gilgamesh:) "My friend hath seen a vision that none shall equal."

On the day he saw the dream his strength was spent.
Enkidu lay stricken, sick one day and a second,
Enkidu lay upon his bed, and his sickness grew heavier.
A third day and a fourth the sickness of Enkidu grew heavier,
a fifth, a sixth and a seventh, an eighth, a ninth and a tenth,
an eleventh and a twelfth day Enkidu lay upon the bed.
He called Gilgamesh and spake:
(Enkidu:) "My god hath spurned me, my friend,
I die not like one that falleth amid the battle.
I feared the fight,
my friend, he that falleth in the fight maketh himself a name,
but I fall not in the fight and make myself no name."
